======================================================================
CHEATSHEET -- what this project actually is, in plain words
======================================================================

WHAT WE'RE BUILDING
--------------------
A PLL (phase-locked loop) is a circuit that takes a slow, cheap, accurate
clock (like a crystal, "REF") and multiplies it up to a faster clock your
chip actually needs -- while keeping that faster clock locked in step with
the slow one. Basically every chip with wireless, video, or a fast digital
core has one.

The catch: the fast part (the VCO, "voltage controlled oscillator") drifts
with temperature. Hot chip = wrong frequency, unless you compensate for it.
That's the "temperature-compensated" part of the title.

WHY OPEN SOURCE / SKY130
--------------------------
Normally you'd design this in Cadence Virtuoso, a very expensive commercial
tool. We don't have a license at home. So instead we used:
  - sky130 = a real, free chip-manufacturing process (SkyWater 130nm) that
    Google/SkyWater made public. It's not a toy -- real chips get made on it.
  - ngspice = a free circuit simulator (does the same job as Cadence's
    "Spectre" simulator, just free and command-line only, no pretty GUI).
  - We wrote the circuits as plain text files (SPICE netlists) by hand,
    instead of drawing them in a schematic editor, because we have no GUI
    tool set up. It's more typing, but it's the exact same circuit.

Magic and KLayout (also free) will be used later for drawing the physical
chip layout (the actual shapes that get manufactured).

THE ACTUAL RESEARCH QUESTION (not just "build a PLL")
--------------------------------------------------------
Anyone can build a temperature-compensated PLL, it's been done and published
already (we're not claiming that part is new). Our angle: when you're
CANCELLING two big drifting numbers against each other to get a tiny leftover
number (that's literally what "compensation" is), any small error in your
simulator gets AMPLIFIED. Nobody has actually measured whether the free
open-source tools are trustworthy enough to design that kind of cancellation
circuit. We're measuring it, by building the same circuit twice -- once free
(ngspice/sky130) and once in real Cadence/Spectre later -- and comparing.

If they agree: good news for everyone who can't afford Cadence.
If they disagree, especially more than expected: that's ALSO a real,
publishable result (tells people how far they can trust the free tools).

WHAT WE ACTUALLY BUILT SO FAR (Review 1, first 50%)
------------------------------------------------------
  REF (a clock) -> PFD -> Charge Pump -> Loop Filter -> VCO -> divide-by-8 -+
                    ^                                                       |
                    +---------------------------------------------------- -+

  - VCO: the oscillator. Ring of 5 inverters chasing their own tail.
    Runs faster when you feed it more voltage (Vctrl).
  - PFD (phase-frequency detector): compares "REF ticked" vs "the VCO's
    output, divided down, ticked" and says "speed up" (UP) or "slow down"
    (DOWN).
  - Charge pump: turns UP/DOWN into a tiny current that nudges a
    capacitor's voltage up or down.
  - Loop filter: just resistors/capacitors that smooth that voltage out
    so it doesn't jerk around.
  - Divider: counts the VCO's fast ticks and outputs 1 tick per 8 VCO
    ticks, so the PFD can compare it fairly against the slower REF.

  We built ALL of these as real transistors (not shortcuts) EXCEPT the
  charge pump, which we made "ideal" on purpose for now (a placeholder
  current source) -- that's a normal, standard thing to do early on, real
  transistor switches come later once we're measuring manufacturing
  imperfections (mismatch), which is a Review 2 thing, not Review 1.

DID IT WORK?
-------------
Yes. The loop LOCKS -- meaning after you turn it on, the VCO's voltage
settles down and stops changing, and the VCO's frequency matches what it's
supposed to (226 MHz-ish, 8x a 28.275MHz reference). It took 577
nanoseconds to settle. The spec said it had to happen in under 2000
nanoseconds (2 microseconds). We're comfortably inside budget.

We also checked the math (a pen-and-paper formula for how fast a PLL
*should* lock) predicted ~450ns. Measured 577ns. Same ballpark -- good,
that means our simulation isn't lying to us, it roughly agrees with
theory.

THE BIG NUMBERS, TRANSLATED
------------------------------
  K_VCO ~ 670 MHz per Volt
    -> "how much does the oscillator speed up per extra volt you feed it."
       Not a constant, it curves -- feed it more, it speeds up less and less
       (diminishing returns), like pressing a gas pedal that's less
       responsive the harder you push.

  Uncompensated drift: 332 to 1070 ppm per degree C, depending on how the
  manufacturing came out (called a "corner" -- ff=fast chips, tt=typical,
  ss=slow chips, think of it like "best case / average / worst case" from
  manufacturing variation, not from temperature itself)
    -> without any fix, the oscillator's frequency changes by that many
       parts-per-million for every 1 degree C change. Over a >3x range
       depending on manufacturing luck. This is the drift our (not yet
       built) compensation circuit needs to cancel out.

  ppm = parts per million. 1 ppm drift on a 1,000,000 Hz signal = drifts
  by 1 Hz. It's just a relative-error unit, like saying "0.0001%" but
  friendlier to say out loud.

THINGS WE CAUGHT AND FIXED (worth remembering, these were real bugs)
------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. A polarity bug: ngspice's way of saying "push this much current into
   this wire" is backwards from what you'd guess. Found it by testing on
   a single capacitor before touching the real circuit -- 30 seconds
   instead of another 3-minute wrong guess.
2. We told the simulator "start this voltage at 0.85V" but forgot to also
   tell it to start a SECOND, related capacitor at a matching voltage.
   Physics doesn't care what we meant -- it just redistributed the charge
   between the two caps and our circuit's starting voltage collapsed to
   basically nothing before the real circuit even got a chance to work.
   One-line fix once we found it. First guess at the cause (a "digital
   glitch") was wrong, and we proved it was wrong before chasing the real
   cause -- that's a good habit, not a bad sign.

SPECS WE INHERITED AND HAD TO CORRECT
-----------------------------------------
  "The output must be accurate to +-0.15 ppm" -- doesn't actually mean
  anything for THIS kind of PLL, because once it's locked, the output
  frequency is set exactly by the reference crystal and a whole-number
  divider, not by anything we're designing. It's like specifying how
  accurate a ruler is when the ruler's accuracy was never in question --
  we replaced it with a number that actually measures what our circuit
  contributes (the VCO's own drift, not the crystal's).

  "The control voltage must be able to go as low as 0.27V" -- turns out
  physically impossible for this oscillator design (the transistor
  literally can't turn on that low). Not a mistake in our circuit, it's a
  real physical limit of this approach. We adjusted the spec to match
  reality (0.63V) instead of pretending the impossible number was fine.
